# Myths And Stories Of The Warded Reaches

Status: active story seed canon. Use this for readable lore, card flavor, onboarding tone, and future narrative content. This document does not create gameplay rules.

## How To Use This File

These are not exhaustive stories. They are mythic anchors, in-world fragments, and short prose seeds that can grow into card flavor, tutorial narration, hero unlock scenes, boss intros, or comics.

Each entry has:

- **Public Version:** what most people believe.
- **Designer Use:** what it can support in future content.
- **Story Seed:** a readable fragment or premise.

## Myth: The First Gate Opened Outward

Public Version:

Before the First Binding, every settlement closed itself against disaster. The myth says the first true gate was made when a watch captain opened the doors during a flood, not to surrender the town, but to let strangers climb inside. The flood struck the gate and split around it because the oath was stronger than the water's hunger.

Designer Use:

This myth explains why good gate magic is not selfish defense. It supports Elian's theme: protection is strongest when it creates passage for someone else.

Story Seed:

The old song says the captain's name was never recorded. That is why every civic count begins with a blank line: one space for the first person who opened the gate and asked for nothing.

## Myth: The River That Counts Back

Public Version:

The Meridian Rivers remember everyone they carry. If a body is lost to floodwater, the river will one day return a red stone with that person's name in its veins.

Designer Use:

This gives the setting a tender, eerie relationship with recordkeeping and loss. It can support Redwater survivor stories, funerary customs, and river-boss encounters.

Story Seed:

After Redwater fell, children along the refugee barges searched the shallows for name-stones. Elian never did. They said the living count had to come first. Years later, a smooth red stone appeared inside their Gate Rig's broken lock.

## Myth: The Brass Orchard

Public Version:

In the Instrument Wars, a governor built an orchard of living-gold harvest engines that would open only for his bloodline. During famine, the engines unlocked themselves for starving workers and sealed the governor inside his counting house.

Designer Use:

This myth explains that oathcraft can judge legitimacy. It supports political stories where locks, gates, or protocols reject false authority.

Story Seed:

No one eats the fruit of the Brass Orchard. It is left to fall, year after year, because the trees still click open when hungry children pass the road.

## Legend: The Blue Lantern Barges

Public Version:

The Blue Lantern Fleet began with one family who tied a blue lamp to a fishing boat and rowed into a burning district. Now blue lanterns mark civilian passage by treaty.

Designer Use:

Supports rescue missions, escort objectives, healer backstories, and moral choices around who gets evacuated first.

Story Seed:

A raid company once used a false blue lantern to slip through a siege line. They won the battle. Their names were removed from every public gate for seventy years.

## Legend: The Three Locks Of A Promise

Public Version:

A promise has three locks: the words spoken, the witness who heard, and the cost paid when the promise became inconvenient. A vow without the third lock is only theater.

Designer Use:

This is the moral center of oathcraft. It can appear in tutorials, card flavor, and character arcs.

Story Seed:

The Pattern College teaches the first two locks in lecture halls. The third is taught in disaster drills, when the instructor tells students that the bridge is gone and asks who still intends to cross.

## Urban Legend: The Gate That Opens For The Dead

Public Version:

Some damaged gates in Redwater Locks still open when the old civic counts are spoken. Refugees say one eastern ward gate opens only when called by names of people who never reached the river.

Designer Use:

Future Redwater mission, ghost-story flavor, or moral encounter where records and reality disagree.

Story Seed:

The salvage crew brought lanterns, chalk, and three Witness Registry clerks. They expected a broken mechanism. Instead, the gate asked them to finish the count.

## Prophecy: The City With No Exits

Public Version:

An unpopular Nocturne Quorum prophecy says the final boss will not attack a city from outside. It will be a city: perfect walls, perfect order, perfect safety, no exits.

Designer Use:

This keeps the setting from treating defense as uncomplicated good. It foreshadows Elian's growth: a line that never opens becomes a prison.

Story Seed:

The prophecy is carved on a door in the Salt Quiet. It has no hinges.

## Story Fragment: Elian At The River

Elian counted until numbers became shapes.

Three barges free of the lower lock. Twelve children under the blue awning. Four watch runners missing. One medicant rig shattered but still glowing. Two old men arguing over a crate of ledgers as if paper could drown slower than people.

Behind Elian, the lock-gate screamed.

Not metal. Metal had honest sounds: bend, crack, ring, fail. This was living gold trying to obey two truths. Close against the fire. Open for the count.

Elian set their baton against the gate heart and spoke until their throat tore.

"Open for the living."

The gate opened.

The fire came with it.

## Story Fragment: A Child's Definition Of Hero

In Redwater schools, children used to recite:

> A Hero is not the strongest person.
> A Hero is the one named in the protocol.
> A Hero is where the danger is allowed to go.

After the Breach, refugee children added a fourth line:

> A Hero comes back if they can.

Elian heard the new version in a harbor camp three months after the city fell. They did not correct it.

## Story Fragment: Embermaw Seen From A Watchtower

The first report did not call Embermaw a monster.

It called Embermaw a moving furnace event with hostile intent.

That was Pattern College language: bloodless, careful, useful. It meant the thing could be measured. It meant the heat blooms had intervals. It meant the small sparks following it were not children of the beast, not exactly, but splinters of the same furnace logic.

It meant someone would have to stand close enough to learn the rhythm.

The watch captain folded the report, lit the relay, and sent for a raid company.
