# Disclose Boss Beats in stages across three Timeline horizons

The Boss Timeline gains a third horizon. The `Forecast Row` previews the next Round's whole Boss Program at family level — its title and the union of its counter tags — while `Incoming` and `Instant` keep their existing positional meanings. What changes alongside the new row is the disclosure contract on a Beat: a Beat states its family and counter tags in the Forecast Row, and every parameter needed by its resolution once it reaches the Incoming or Instant Row. A Beat is therefore legally incomplete in one row, which is the surprising part and the reason this is an ADR rather than a bible section.

This does not supersede ADR 0024. A Forecast Row never resolves — it is next Round's program shown a Round early, not a fourth resolution step — so "resolve a Boss Row in the batch that opens its window" is untouched, and the per-Round event order stays as 0024 fixed it.

The reason to add a horizon rather than stage information inside the existing Incoming Row is that our two rows both live inside one Round. The cooperative-boss research proposes a `T+2 → T+1 → T` maturation pipeline in which the outer state names the *kind* of problem developing and the inner state carries the concrete parameters, so a party can reserve resources for a category before it can answer a specific hit. Under ADR 0024 our Incoming Row buys one player window of lead, not a Round, so that pipeline cannot be obtained by renaming rows. Staging the disclosure inside the Incoming Row was considered and rejected: one window of deliberate vagueness is a readability tax rather than a planning horizon, and it makes a single row mean two different things depending on when the player looks at it. Forecasting each Beat individually was also rejected — it discloses essentially the whole next Round, which leaves the complete-disclosure state with no job — as was forecasting only the highest-consequence Beat, which hides exactly the interaction between Beats that the party needs to plan against. Forecasting one whole Boss Program is also the option that costs no new authoring concept, because the Program is already the authored unit.

The row is what makes Telegraph Proportionality enforceable. That rule becomes a three-tier ladder in the Embermaw design doc — `Chip` may originate in any row, `Structural` no later than Incoming, `Severe` must appear in the Forecast Row first — and the escape hatch that let a top-tier hit ship as an Instant with a written justification is removed, because the horizon it lacked now exists. The tier ladder and the row stand or fall together, and D-021 records them as one decision for that reason.

Two consequences are accepted openly. First, the Forecast Row carries little information for an experienced player while Embermaw's `boss_programs` rotation loops deterministically: after one loop, next Round's program is already known. We build the row anyway, because the Severe tier has no mechanism without it and because the slice's audience is first-time players — the Briefing publishes the move catalog but never the order, so the Forecast Row is the only surface that tells a new player *when*. That redundancy is recorded as the standing argument for the Module Slot of D-024, not as an argument against the row. Second, the Briefing and the Forecast Row are defined as non-overlapping by construction: the Briefing is the catalog of what a Boss can do, the Forecast Row is the schedule, and rotation order stays unbriefed so first-attempt discovery survives. Acceptance is D-021's gate — Timeline conversion rate observed over a party's first three attempts, testing whether the row is actionable information or decorative UI.

**Shipped 2026-08-17.** The row is a pure projection, `forecast(catalog, state)`, the same shape as `minionIntents`: it reads the program the rotation will select next and returns its title, the union of its counter tags in first-appearance order, and its highest Consequence Tier. Nothing resolves and nothing mutates, so the per-Round event order and every committed Scenario are untouched. The tier joins the title and tags on the row because severity is what tells a party how much to hold in reserve — the research's own Forecast example carries it — and the strip renders the whole thing as one dashed chip plus secondary tag text, deliberately the faintest row on the strip.

The tiers became authored data (`consequence_tier` on every Beat) rather than derived values, because "can down a Hero" depends on Hero health and would be fragile to compute. What is enforced instead is the ladder's implications, as tests over live content: a Beat that can add Escalation must be authored `severe`; a Beat that spawns a Minion or leaves a Hazard must be at least `structural`; and the first program in an encounter's rotation may carry no `severe` Beat.

That last rule is the ladder's one honest hole. Every Beat in every program is forecast a Round ahead automatically once the row exists — except in Round 1, where no earlier Round could have shown it. Rather than special-case the row at the pull, Round 1 is simply not forecast, the Encounter Briefing covers what the Boss can do beforehand, and the constraint moves to content: a first program stays free of `severe` Beats. Embermaw satisfies this today without effort, because it has no `severe` Beat at all — the tier is enforced but unexercised until content earns it, which is the honest state to be in rather than inventing a `severe` Beat to justify the mechanism.
