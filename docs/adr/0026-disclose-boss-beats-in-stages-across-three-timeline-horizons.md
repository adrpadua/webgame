# Disclose Boss Beats in stages across three Timeline horizons

> **Superseded 2026-08-18 by [ADR 0031](0031-remove-the-forecast-row-and-let-the-schedule-be-learned.md), in the half that added the row.** This ADR set its own acceptance gate — "whether the row is actionable information or decorative UI" — and the gate was run: across 200 seeds per position, a policy that read the Forecast Row and one blind to it finished in the same Round to two decimal places, with identical checkpoint rates. The row and `forecast()` are deleted. The disclosure contract for `Incoming` and `Instant`, and everything this ADR says about not superseding ADR 0024, still hold. The Consequence Tier ladder survives in reduced form: `Severe must appear in the Forecast Row first` is void, while the rule that a phase's first program carries no `Severe` Beat is retained and now load-bearing. The claim below that the ladder and the row "stand or fall together" is the specific thing ADR 0031 revises.


The Boss Timeline gains a third horizon. The `Forecast Row` previews the next Round's whole Boss Program at family level — its title and the union of its counter tags — while `Incoming` and `Instant` keep their existing positional meanings. What changes alongside the new row is the disclosure contract on a Beat: a Beat states its family and counter tags in the Forecast Row, and every parameter needed by its resolution once it reaches the Incoming or Instant Row. A Beat is therefore legally incomplete in one row, which is the surprising part and the reason this is an ADR rather than a bible section.

This does not supersede ADR 0024. A Forecast Row never resolves — it is next Round's program shown a Round early, not a fourth resolution step — so "resolve a Boss Row in the batch that opens its window" is untouched, and the per-Round event order stays as 0024 fixed it.

The reason to add a horizon rather than stage information inside the existing Incoming Row is that our two rows both live inside one Round. The cooperative-boss research proposes graduated certainty: at long range reveal the mechanic *family*, at medium range the exact ability and board region, and only at short range lock targets and magnitude — so a party can reserve resources for a category before it can answer a specific hit. Under ADR 0024 our Incoming Row buys one player window of lead, not a Round, so that pipeline cannot be obtained by renaming rows. Staging the disclosure inside the Incoming Row was considered and rejected: one window of deliberate vagueness is a readability tax rather than a planning horizon, and it makes a single row mean two different things depending on when the player looks at it. Forecasting each Beat individually was also rejected — it discloses essentially the whole next Round, which leaves the complete-disclosure state with no job — as was forecasting only the highest-consequence Beat, which hides exactly the interaction between Beats that the party needs to plan against. Forecasting one whole Boss Program is also the option that costs no new authoring concept, because the Program is already the authored unit.

The row is what makes Telegraph Proportionality enforceable. That rule becomes a three-tier ladder in the Embermaw design doc — `Chip` may originate in any row, `Structural` no later than Incoming, `Severe` must appear in the Forecast Row first — and the escape hatch that let a top-tier hit ship as an Instant with a written justification is removed, because the horizon it lacked now exists. The tier ladder and the row stand or fall together, and D-021 records them as one decision for that reason.

Two consequences are accepted openly. First, the Forecast Row carries little information for an experienced player while Embermaw's `boss_programs` rotation loops deterministically: after one loop, next Round's program is already known. We build the row anyway, because the Severe tier has no mechanism without it and because the slice's audience is first-time players — the Briefing publishes the move catalog but never the order, so the Forecast Row is the only surface that tells a new player *when*. That redundancy is recorded as the standing argument for the Module Slot of D-024, not as an argument against the row. Second, the Briefing and the Forecast Row are defined as non-overlapping by construction: the Briefing is the catalog of what a Boss can do, the Forecast Row is the schedule, and rotation order stays unbriefed so first-attempt discovery survives. Acceptance is D-021's gate — Timeline conversion rate observed over a party's first three attempts, testing whether the row is actionable information or decorative UI.

**Shipped 2026-08-17.** The row is a pure projection, `forecast(catalog, state)`, the same shape as `minionIntents`: it reads the program the rotation will select next and returns its title, the union of its counter tags in first-appearance order, and its highest Consequence Tier. Nothing resolves and nothing mutates, so the per-Round event order and every committed Scenario are untouched. The tier joins the title and tags on the row because severity is what tells a party how much to hold in reserve — the research's own Forecast example carries it — and the strip renders the whole thing as one dashed chip plus secondary tag text, deliberately the faintest row on the strip.

The tiers became authored data (`consequence_tier` on every Beat) rather than derived values, because "can down a Hero" depends on Hero health and would be fragile to compute. What is enforced instead is the ladder's implications, as tests over live content: a Beat that can add Escalation must be authored `severe`; a Beat that spawns a Minion or leaves a Hazard must be at least `structural`; and the first program in an encounter's rotation may carry no `severe` Beat.

That last rule is the ladder's one honest hole. Every Beat in every program is forecast a Round ahead automatically once the row exists — except in Round 1, where no earlier Round could have shown it. Rather than special-case the row at the pull, Round 1 is simply not forecast, the Encounter Briefing covers what the Boss can do beforehand, and the constraint moves to content: a first program stays free of `severe` Beats. Embermaw satisfies this today without effort, because it has no `severe` Beat at all — the tier is enforced but unexercised until content earns it, which is the honest state to be in rather than inventing a `severe` Beat to justify the mechanism.

**Amended 2026-08-17 (D-036, D-037): both accepted consequences came due, and both were paid.**

The first accepted consequence — "the Forecast Row carries little information for an experienced player while Embermaw's rotation loops deterministically" — turned out to understate the problem. The row carried little information for a *first-time* player too, and not because of the rotation alone. Measured over live content, all five of Embermaw's programs were the same six Beats: `hunt` and `brood` were identical field-for-field except `id`, `title`, and flavour text, and every program's counter-tag union was the same five tags. So the row's tag line was a constant. A horizon that always reads `Position, Mitigate, Move, Interrupt, Kill Adds` is not staged disclosure; it is a label.

Two changes fixed the two halves. ADR 0028 draws the program order from the seed, so *when* a program lands is no longer deducible from the Round number. And D-036 gave each Phase I program a distinct demand, so *what* the row names now varies: Hunt asks for Mitigate and Move, Ember asks for Move only — Armor is wasted on it — and Brood is the only program that asks anyone to kill something. A test asserts the three tag sets stay pairwise distinct, because that property, not the row's existence, is what makes the horizon actionable.

The second consequence was the tier ladder being "enforced but unexercised until content earns it." Content earned it. Brood Call is priced now (ADR 0027's D-036 amendment), and a Beat that can add Escalation is `severe` by definition, so the Severe tier has a live occupant and reaches the Forecast Row exactly as the ladder specifies. That also promoted the ladder's honest hole from one case to two: Round 1 was never forecast, and neither is the Phase Break. The rule generalises rather than gaining an exception — the first program of *every* phase must be free of `severe` Beats — and Embermaw's `phase_two_programs` was reordered to `ashfall, molting` to satisfy it.

D-021's acceptance gate is unchanged and still outstanding: Timeline conversion rate over a party's first three attempts is a human measurement, and nothing here supplies it. What has changed is that the row now has different values to convert.

**Measured 2026-08-17 (D-038): the row is not actionable in the solo slice, and that is a finding about the fight rather than about the row.**

D-021's acceptance gate — Timeline conversion rate over a party's first three attempts — needs humans. A mechanical stand-in exists now: two policies identical in every respect except that one consults `forecast()` before spending its Slow Window on Fortify. Fortify is the only card whose payoff lands next Round (D-019 banks its Armor for the next Round *start*), so the Forecast Row is the only surface that can price that spend. A program wanting `Mitigate` opens with a Raking Claw in its Instant Row, a hit no in-Round action can answer, which is exactly what banked Armor is for; a program that does not deals its damage through a dodgeable cone instead.

The reader gained nothing. At 200 seeds, `far` and `dodge` are **identical** to their blind twins on every outcome — same average Round, same spread, same final Escalation, same checkpoint rate — despite the reader withholding its Slow spend about 1.8 times per run. On `stay` the reader was *worse*: 59% Health deaths against 47%.

Two causes, and both are worth keeping.

The first is that these runs end at Escalation, not at zero Health, and **mitigation quality does not touch the clock**. Armor decisions cannot reach the outcome, so the information has no lever to pull. That is a statement about Embermaw as currently tuned: it has exactly one decision that reaches the ending — clear the adds or do not — and every mitigation choice is inert. Enriching the Forecast Row cannot fix that, because the row would still be pricing a spend that changes nothing.

The second is subtler and is a genuine constraint on the row's contract. **Counter tags describe what a program can be answered with, not what the reader is able to execute.** `stay` cannot move, so Ember Pattern's "dodgeable" cone is not dodgeable *for it*, and Armor was the right answer all along — the reader acted on "Ember does not want `Mitigate`", which is true of the program and false of that Hero. Family-level disclosure is only actionable to a party that can perform the named answer, which means the row's value scales with kit breadth. A solo Tank is close to the worst possible reader, and a real party is close to the best.

So this does not retire the row, and it is not evidence that ADR 0026 was wrong. It is evidence that the row cannot be justified from the solo slice, and it reorders what to build: **make mitigation reach the outcome before making the Forecast Row richer.** The Module Slot (D-024) would add information to a horizon whose information is currently free to ignore, and it should wait until this differential can move.
