# Randomize before the window that answers it

No rules-relevant random choice may resolve after the player window that answers it. When a mechanic's parameters are chosen at random — impact hexes, add placement, which Hero a selector lands on, which module fills a slot — that choice resolves at or before the moment the mechanic becomes visible enough to plan against, and the parameters the party then reads are the parameters that resolve. The prohibited shape is the mirror image: players commit mitigation and movement, and only then does the game roll to decide whether the commitment mattered.

This is the placement rule that ADR 0012 left open. That ADR made random choices seeded and reproducible, which is a property of the *stream*; it says nothing about *when* a draw may happen, so a post-commitment roll would satisfy it completely while producing the worst play experience the cooperative-boss research identifies. The research frames the distinction as the difference between a fair raid and an unfair-feeling one: both randomize, but only pre-commitment randomization produces a learning loop, because only there can a wipe be narrated as a decision the party could have made differently. Its named failure signal is a playtest in which the answer to "what would have prevented this" is repeatedly "nothing, bad draw" — which is also why D-027 makes failure attribution a required metric rather than leaving this law unmeasured.

The law is cheap to adopt now and expensive later, which is the whole reason to write it down at this moment. The authored Embermaw encounter currently resolves with no rules randomness at all: `brood_call` takes the first empty authored candidates in board order rather than drawing from `brood_spawn_candidates` at random, and the seeded generator built for ADR 0012 drives no Beat. So today the rule forbids nothing and constrains everything that comes next — most sharply the Module Slot of D-024, whose selection must be settled before the Forecast Row announces the module's family, and per-Beat parameter randomization, which was rejected as a module granularity precisely because it is the shape that tempts an author into rolling at resolution.

We rejected the softer version — "prefer" pre-commitment randomness, with exceptions permitted for a Beat that argues for them — because a preference is not checkable and the exception would be claimed by exactly the mechanics that most need the rule. We also rejected forbidding randomness outright: bounded variation is what makes an encounter worth replaying after mastery, and Aeon's End and Spirit Island both demonstrate uncertainty that pressures planning without punishing it. Acceptance is a content-schema check that no authored random draw is scheduled after its answering window, run alongside the existing content gates.
